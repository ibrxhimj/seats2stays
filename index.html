02 · The response

10% more passengers bring about 3% more check-ins

Assuming one passenger equals one hotel guest overstates the effect. On the official test window it forecast worse than assuming nothing changes.

We measured the real response across 33 countries, comparing each country's month with the same month a year earlier and controlling for airlines adding seats ahead of demand. The answer is an elasticity of ε = 0.31 (80% range 0.20–0.43): check-ins grow much less than one for one.

Stage B · check-ins to guest nights

Discrete-time proportional hazards (Cox, 1972; Prentice & Gloeckler, 1978)

Tonight's guests are recent arrivals who haven't checked out yet. Each nationality has its own checkout pattern, scaled by the calendar: checkouts are 23% more likely on Sundays, and summer stays are longer.

06 · For DCT

Grow hotel stays, not just seats

  • Set a target, get the flights. 10,000 more guest nights from the UK this winter needs about 4 more flights a week, with a range.
  • Rank routes by hotel nights per seat. In the model, a seat's value differs up to 14-fold between markets.
  • Time capacity for the strong months. A seat added in April is worth about half as much again as one in June.
  • Test any deal before signing. Routes, frequencies, aircraft, load factor or connecting share, by nationality, month and day.

Model estimates with 80% ranges.

07 · Next step

A 90-day pilot with DCT

  1. Blind test the frozen model on DCT's newest hotel data.
  2. Connect monthly hotel statistics and airline schedules.
  3. Shadow real 2027 route and event decisions.

It runs offline on one machine; about one analyst, a few hours a month.

08 · What we would add

Where it goes next

  • Live updating: monthly refits and a Kalman filter that updates forecasts as hotels report.
  • Hotel supply and prices: room inventory and rates, to flag scenarios beyond capacity.
  • Market-by-market responses: sharpened as new routes build up known outcomes.
